\begin{abstract}
Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting is often expected to help reasoning in any language. We test this on date and time arithmetic with Sarvam-105B, using 6,750 questions from the Temporal Reasoning Dataset in English and Hindi. CoT helps English a great deal (+29.5 points) and Hindi very little (+4.7), so the gap between the languages grows from about 4 points to about 29. Most of this gap follows the language the model reasons in, not the language of the question: asking for English reasoning on the same Hindi questions closes about four-fifths of it. A two-turn cross-lingual prompt closes about a third. Asking for Hinglish reasoning closes none of it on Hindi questions and costs English questions 18.0 points. An error analysis ties the Hindi losses to methods the model uses only when it reasons in Hindi, such as subtracting dates by counting forward. The more differently the model reasons in the two languages, the more likely the languages are to disagree on correctness (odds ratio 4.93 per 0.1 of divergence), even after controlling for trace length, though this is only an association. Editing or resampling traces that mention a distractor cannot show that the distractor caused the error. Everything comes from one model with one prompt per condition, so we report patterns and do not claim mechanisms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting asks a language model to write intermediate steps before answering, and it can improve multi-step reasoning \citep{wei-etal-2022-chain,kojima2022large}. Whether the benefit carries across languages is less settled. Work on MGSM found English intermediate steps as good as or better than steps in the question's language \citep{shi-etal-2023-mgsm}. If that holds, a model asked to reason in Hindi may do worse even when it reads the Hindi question well.

We test this on temporal reasoning using the Temporal Reasoning Dataset (TRD) of \citet{mazzia-etal-2026-benchmarking}, which turns CoT off and leaves its effect to future work. TRD can also prepend a distractor sentence to any question, which lets us ask whether reasoning out loud gives irrelevant text more room to do harm, as irrelevant sentences do on English arithmetic \citep{shi-etal-2023-distracted}.

We use Sarvam-105B, whose developer reports giving a substantial part of the training budget to the ten most-spoken Indian languages \citep{sarvam-2026-models}, and ask six questions:
\begin{description}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{1pt}
\item[RQ1] Does CoT help, and does that depend on language and difficulty?
\item[RQ2] Does CoT make distractors more harmful?
\item[RQ3] How much of the English-Hindi gap follows the language of reasoning rather than the language of the question?
\item[RQ4] Does asking for Hinglish reasoning help?
\item[RQ5] Does divergence between English and Hindi traces track whether the two languages disagree?
\item[RQ6] Can we definitively say that the distractors in the CoT traces cause errors?
\end{description}

In short, CoT helps English far more than Hindi, and switching only the reasoning language to English recovers most of the difference; the errors left over come mostly from reading the Hindi question. Hinglish does not help. The other answers are more cautious. Distractor harm is small and concentrated in one sentence, trace divergence goes with disagreement but only as a correlation, and our interventions cannot pin errors on the distractor. Table~\ref{tab:summary} lists each answer with its headline number.

Our contributions are: (1) to our knowledge, the first CoT evaluation on TRD, split by distractor condition; (2) a decomposition of the gap into reasoning and question language, backed by trace structure and by the types of errors; (3) a question-clustered regression showing that divergence between English and Hindi traces is strongly associated with the two languages disagreeing, beyond trace length; and (4) trace-deletion and on-policy resampling experiments on distractor mentions, with their limits stated. We recomputed every reported number from raw responses and corrected our earlier drafts where they overclaimed.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{CoT and when it stops helping.}
\citet{wei-etal-2022-chain} introduced CoT with worked examples, and \citet{kojima2022large} showed a trigger phrase alone can work, which suits TRD because it has no worked solutions. \citet{sprague2025cot} find CoT helps mainly on math and symbolic problems, so date arithmetic should benefit. \citet{liu2025mind} show it can lower performance on some tasks, so a benefit on every task cannot be assumed.

\paragraph{Distractors.}
GSM-IC adds one irrelevant sentence to GSM8K problems, and accuracy falls under several prompting methods \citep{shi-etal-2023-distracted}. GSM-Symbolic shows sensitivity to clauses that look relevant but are not \citep{mirzadeh2025gsmsymbolic}. Neither compares CoT on and off under the same distractor, and both work in English.

\paragraph{Language of reasoning.}
MGSM compares native and English CoT with few-shot examples on ten languages that do not include Hindi \citep{shi-etal-2023-mgsm}. Cross-lingual prompting (CLP) adds an understanding turn before the solving turn \citep{qin-etal-2023-cross}, and cross-lingual thought prompting reports over 10 points of average improvement on arithmetic reasoning and open-domain question answering \citep{huang-etal-2023-languages}. Thinking models often revert to English, and forcing the user's language can cost accuracy \citep{qi-etal-2025-models}; such models may also reason better in high-resource languages \citep{yong2025crosslingual}. Interpretability work suggests English-leaning intermediate processing in other models \citep{wendler-etal-2024-llamas,zhao2024handle}; none of this work covers Sarvam-105B. Hinglish is an established code-switching pair \citep{khanuja-etal-2020-gluecos,aguilar-etal-2020-lince}, and code-mixed examples can help on some tasks \citep{shankar-etal-2024-context}, though these studies use code-mixed input and do not test generated reasoning.

\paragraph{Traces and faithfulness.}
A trace need not record the model's computation \citep{turpin-etal-2023-unfaithful}, so faithfulness is tested by intervening on it \citep{lanham-etal-2023-measuring}. Thought Anchors resamples continuations to estimate each sentence's counterfactual impact \citep{bogdan-etal-2025-anchors}, and Thought Branches argues that hand edits are off-policy and, in decision tasks, have smaller and less stable effects than resampling \citep{macar-etal-2026-branches}. Models can also keep following an injected thought \citep{yang-etal-2025-unhelpful}. Step quality has been scored directly \citep{golovneva2023roscoe,prasad-etal-2023-receval}; our divergence score is structural and does not assess validity.

\paragraph{Temporal benchmarks.}
TimeBench and related work evaluate temporal reasoning mostly in English \citep{chu-etal-2024-timebench,wang-zhao-2024-tram}. TRD adds ten languages and controlled distractors \citep{mazzia-etal-2026-benchmarking}. We did not find a study crossing CoT, distractors and reasoning language on the same problems, though our search was not systematic.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Data.}
TRD has nine tasks: date addition, subtraction and duration, time addition, subtraction and duration, date recurrence, interval date (the start and end of next week) and day of week. We generated 150 questions per task at each of five difficulty levels, 6,750 in all, in English and Hindi with matching IDs. Each question appears three times: with no distractor, with a time-related one and with an unrelated one, drawn from pools of 35 and 43 sentence pairs. We recomputed all 13,500 gold answers with Python's \texttt{datetime} and all match.

\paragraph{Prompting conditions.}
All runs use temperature 0, thinking off, a 4,096-token limit and a fixed answer line so responses can be scored automatically. Figure~\ref{fig:design} shows one question going through every condition.
\begin{description}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{1pt}
\item[Direct] asks only for the answer.
\item[CoT] asks the model to reason step by step in the question's language (zero-shot, one stage).
\item[en-CoT] keeps the Hindi question and asks for reasoning in English, a zero-shot version of \citet{shi-etal-2023-mgsm}.
\item[CLP] uses the two turns of \citet{qin-etal-2023-cross}: first restate and understand the Hindi problem in English, then solve it. They tune temperature per stage where we fix it at 0, we add a rule to write in English, and we do not run their voting variant (CLSP).
\item[Hinglish] asks for reasoning that mixes Hindi and English in Roman script, on both question languages.
\end{description}
Direct and CoT run on all 6,750 questions in both languages. The other three run on the first 50 questions of each task and level (2,250 questions). A small pilot with thinking mode on (10 questions per cell, \texttt{reasoning\_effort} low, 16,000 tokens) gives one more comparison.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
A response with no answer line usually means the model hit the token limit. \emph{Answered} accuracy leaves such responses out, and our figures use it. \emph{Strict} accuracy counts them as wrong, which is closer to TRD's own scoring \citep{mazzia-etal-2026-benchmarking}. English CoT has the most cut-offs among thinking-off conditions, so answered accuracy flatters it; we give both for headline results and say when a conclusion changes. A distractor's \emph{drop} is accuracy without it minus accuracy with it, and the CoT \emph{penalty} is how much larger that drop is under CoT than under direct answering.

\paragraph{Trace measures.}
For each trace we record its length, whether it contains a verification phrase (found by regex), and whether it mentions the distractor, compared with a placebo rate on the same question without the distractor. For Hindi traces, the target-language ratio (TLR) is the share of Devanagari words among Devanagari and Latin-script words outside the answer line. A script also labels every wrong response, 30,421 of the 93,150 we collected, by how far off it is, the kind of mistake (such as one day off, wrong direction or whole hours off) and what the question involves (such as a month end or 29 February); cut-offs count as wrong there.

\paragraph{Comparing traces.}
For RQ5 a regex parser turns each trace into a sequence of operation tokens with their operands, such as \texttt{SUB 2025-05-26 4} for ``4 days before 26 May 2025''. The reasoning divergence score (RDS) between two traces is the Levenshtein distance \citep{levenshtein1966binary} between their sequences divided by the longer length, so 0 means the same steps in the same order and 1 means no step in common. The parser covers 95--96\% of CoT segments on standard replies and 86--88\% on thinking-mode reasoning. On 240 tokens read by one LLM annotator, not validated by a person, it gets the operation right 85\% of the time and the operation with its arguments 77\%.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Accuracy intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals \citep{berg-kirkpatrick-etal-2012-empirical} that resample questions as clusters, since a question appears in three distractor conditions. RQ5 odds ratios use question-clustered standard errors, and task-by-level statements use uncorrected intervals. Task-level tests use exact McNemar with Holm correction over nine tasks \citep{mcnemar-1947-note,holm-1979-simple,dror-etal-2018-hitchhikers}, and mention associations use Mantel-Haenszel odds ratios \citep{mantel-haenszel-1959-statistical}. These intervals treat the distractor sentences and prompts as fixed, so they understate uncertainty about other wordings.

\subsection{RQ1: CoT helps English far more than Hindi}

\textbf{CoT helps English by about 30 points and Hindi by about 5.} Over all 6,750 questions, CoT raises English accuracy by 29.5 points and Hindi by 4.7 (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). The gap between the languages is 4.2 points when the model answers directly and 29.1 with CoT (strict 4.2 and 26.7). It is the same with and without distractors, so pooling does not create it. Without CoT, day-of-week answers are guesses: 85\% of direct answers are wrong (3,839 of 4,500), the rate expected by chance (85.7\%).

\textbf{For Hindi, CoT can make things worse.} Even with no distractor, Hindi CoT lowers accuracy on date subtraction by 19.9 points [16.4, 23.4] and on interval date by 23.3 [19.0, 27.7]. Section~\ref{sec:errors} traces both losses to specific methods. English CoT is never significantly below direct answering on any task, or on any task at any level.

On all 6,750 questions, the gap under CoT grows with difficulty, from 24.0 points on the shortest questions to 34.9 on the longest, but a logistic trend test is not significant ($p=0.21$), so we describe the widening only in points.

\subsection{RQ2: Distractors do little harm, mostly through one sentence}

\textbf{On average the CoT penalty is small.} A time-related distractor lowers Hindi CoT accuracy by 2.6 points [1.3, 3.8], which is 2.2 more than it lowers Hindi direct answers. When the 45 task-sentence units are treated as the sample, however, the interval reaches zero (+2.3 [$-$0.1, 4.6]), so the penalty may not extend to new sentences. Whether Hindi is penalised more than English depends on how unanswered responses are scored (Appendix~B). We therefore withdraw our mid-report claim that distractors make CoT fragile only in Hindi.

\textbf{One sentence does most of the damage.} The largest effect, found post hoc among 90 sentences, comes from the sentence in Figure~\ref{fig:design}: ``The last time I had a weekly commitment was in January.'' On date recurrence it makes the model try to fit January into the question. English CoT runs to the token limit on 92 of 134 questions (4 of 134 without it), and strict English accuracy on those questions falls from 88.8\% to 19.4\%. Hindi has no cut-offs but falls from 26.9\% to 2.2\%. This sentence competes with the start date in the question, so it is not irrelevant in the GSM-IC sense.

\textbf{Hindi traces mention distractors more often.} For time-related sentences, 25.0\% of Hindi traces mention the distractor against 8.5\% of English traces, with a near-zero placebo rate on a word list chosen after seeing the data. Traces that mention a time-related distractor are less often correct (Hindi OR 0.77 [0.67, 0.89]), while unrelated sentences show no such link. RQ6 tests whether the mention is a cause.

\subsection{RQ3: The gap follows the language of reasoning}

\textbf{Switching only the reasoning language to English closes most of the gap.} On the same 2,250 questions, English questions with English CoT score 88.9\%, Hindi questions with English reasoning (en-CoT) 82.9\%, and Hindi questions with Hindi CoT 59.4\% (Table~\ref{tab:accuracy}). Of the 29.5-point gap, 23.5 points lie between en-CoT and Hindi CoT, where only the reasoning language changes. That is 79.7\% of the gap, or 80.7\% under strict scoring. The remaining 6.0 points lie between the question languages, close to the 4.5-point gap under direct answering, where no reasoning is written (Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition}). The reasoning part is larger on every task and in every distractor condition.

\textbf{The traces split the same way.} English CoT and en-CoT traces, which share a reasoning language, are about as far apart (RDS 0.730) as two English traces of one question with and without a distractor (0.713). en-CoT and Hindi CoT traces on identical Hindi questions are as far apart (0.837) as English and Hindi CoT (0.852), and traces of unrelated questions sit at 0.959 (Figure~\ref{fig:distance}). Self-checking follows the reasoning language too: a verification phrase appears in 86.1\% of English CoT traces, 74.2\% of en-CoT traces and 1.3\% of Hindi CoT traces. Verification does not explain the en-CoT gain: an observational estimate within task and level attributes only 1.0\% [$-$6.3, 9.0] of it to verification.

\textbf{Hindi CoT does reason in Hindi.} Its TLR is 0.988, and only 2.14\% of Hindi CoT traces fall below 0.9. Those drifting traces are more often correct within task and level (OR 2.08 [1.66, 2.60]), an observational link that points the same way as en-CoT.

\paragraph{CLP helps less than one English turn.}
CLP scores 70.3\%, 10.9 points above Hindi CoT but 12.6 below en-CoT. This reverses the order \citet{qin-etal-2023-cross} report for GPT-3.5 on MGSM, although model, task and settings all differ. One candidate reason is that the understanding turn often solves the problem already and the solving turn copies it. On the three date-answer tasks, the second turn is right in 97.5\% of responses when the first ends at the gold date and in 20.1\% when it does not. The first turn was not randomised, so this is not proof, and our temperature of 0 and added language rule may also contribute.

\paragraph{Thinking mode.}
With thinking on, Hindi CoT reaches 97.9\% answered (89.9\% strict), and its hidden reasoning is 79\% English words. This fits the account above without confirming it, since thinking mode also had four times the token budget (Appendix~B).

\subsection{RQ4: Hinglish reasoning does not help}

\textbf{It leaves Hindi questions unchanged and makes English ones worse.} Asking for Hinglish reasoning costs English questions 18.0 points [16.8, 19.2] (strict 15.7), on all nine tasks and in each distractor condition (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:hingtasks}). Hindi questions do not change on average ($-$0.0 [$-$1.3, 1.2]), so the gap narrows only because English gets worse.

\textbf{The model mostly ignores the script instruction.} On Hindi questions, 68.5\% of Hinglish traces are mostly Devanagari, so this arm mostly tests code-mixed Devanagari, and the traces that do use Roman script score no better.

\textbf{The English loss goes beyond the cost of writing Hindi.} English-question traces with no Hindi function words still lose 10.3 points [8.3, 12.3], and the share that verify falls from 86.5\% to 18.6\%. One failure matches Hindi CoT: on date subtraction across a month boundary, English questions fall from 90.1\% to 42.3\%, near Hindi CoT's 36.2\%, while same-month questions stay at 97.4\% (Appendix~A has an example).

Errors are concentrated. Day of week, interval date and date recurrence hold 64\% of all wrong answers (19,583 of 30,421), and 78--88\% whenever the model reasons in English, which rarely gets the plain arithmetic wrong. Table~\ref{tab:errors} lists the main patterns.

\textbf{Hindi CoT fails through methods it uses only in Hindi.} Both tasks where Hindi CoT does worse than direct answering (RQ1) have a matching error. On date subtraction it often counts forward to the end of the month and then steps into the next month instead of the previous one, so 13\% of its wrong answers land on or after today. On interval date, a fifth of its wrong answers are ``weeks'' that are not seven days long. en-CoT, which reads the same questions, never walks forward and gives broken weeks only as often as English CoT. Hindi CoT also rarely checks its work: 3.7\% of its traces contain a check or revise step, against 88\% for English CoT and 76\% for en-CoT.

\textbf{en-CoT keeps the errors that come from reading Hindi.} On recurrence, en-CoT counts backwards from today in 28\% of its wrong answers, against 3\% for English CoT, reading \hi{आज को छोड़कर} (`excluding today') as `the occurrence before today'. These answers cluster at $k=1$: about two-thirds of answers to Hindi $k=1$ questions go backwards under en-CoT (67.1\%), Hindi CoT (67.8\%) and CLP (69.0\%), against 3.5\% for English CoT (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:recurrence}). The template's non-standard \hi{1वीं} is a candidate cause, but equally non-standard ordinals at $k=2$ to 4 rarely do this, and we did not rerun a corrected template. en-CoT also reads ``0 hours'' as a number of hours and is wrong on 19\% of such questions, against 5\% on others. So changing the reasoning language fixes what the model writes but not what it reads, the same split as in RQ3.

\textbf{Some errors belong to the model in either language.} On day of week, the most common error under both English CoT and en-CoT is exactly one day late, from counting 1 January itself when adding up the days. Recurrences often stop one interval short because the model counts today as the first occurrence. With 29 February in the span, English CoT on the longest questions is wrong 36\% of the time against 9\% without it, even though 185 of its 190 wrong answers on such questions mention the leap year. Long re-checking loops mark failures: cut-off English CoT traces average 42 check or revise steps, against 8 for wrong traces that finish.

\subsection{RQ5: More divergent traces go with disagreement}

\textbf{Setup.} We pair the English and Hindi CoT traces of each question, distractor condition and thinking mode, and call a pair \emph{disagreeing} when exactly one language is right. A logistic regression predicts disagreement from RDS, controlling for task, difficulty, distractor and thinking mode, with standard errors clustered by question. A second model adds the length gap $|\ell_{en}-\ell_{hi}|/\max(\ell_{en},\ell_{hi})$, since edit distance grows when one trace is longer. The sample is 20,662 pairs, 19,471 with thinking off (Appendix~B).

\textbf{Results.} The languages disagree on 31.4\% [30.7, 32.0] of pairs, and in 92\% of these English is right and Hindi wrong. Disagreeing pairs have more divergent traces: mean RDS is 0.917 against 0.809 for agreeing pairs, and a random disagreeing pair has the higher RDS 80\% of the time (Mann-Whitney $p<0.001$). Under the controls, each 0.1 of RDS multiplies the odds of disagreement by 4.93 [4.58, 5.30], about 23 percentage points on average, with predicted disagreement near zero below RDS 0.7 and climbing steeply above 0.8 (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:lrbins}). Adding the length gap raises the odds ratio to 5.58 [5.18, 6.03], and the gap's own coefficient is negative, so length differences do not explain the link. This is an association, not a cause: RDS and correctness come from the same generations, the outcome mostly tracks Hindi errors, and 88\% of pairs have RDS above 0.7, so the slope rests on the upper range.

\subsection{RQ6: Can we definitively say that the distractors in the CoT traces cause errors?}

RQ2 found that Hindi traces that mention a time-related distractor are less often correct. That could mean the mention leads the model astray, or only that traces already going wrong tend to talk about it. To tell these apart we intervene on the traces.

\paragraph{Which traces.}
We take CoT responses to questions with a time-related distractor that ended in a wrong answer and mention the distractor before answering: 526 Hindi and 37 English. We split each trace into sentences and call the first sentence that mentions the distractor $k$. Each experiment cuts the trace at or before $k$, changes one thing, and lets the model continue writing from there. The Sarvam API always starts a new reply, so we served an FP8 build of the model with vLLM. It scored 5.7 points above the API on 278 Hindi questions and 24.7 below on 22 English ones, so English results are descriptive only.

\paragraph{Experiment 1: deleting the mention.}
On 234 of these traces (222 Hindi), we cut the trace after sentence $k$, or after a matched neutral sentence if that comes later, and build three versions. \emph{A} keeps the text as written, \emph{B} deletes the mention, and \emph{C} deletes a neutral sentence of similar length instead. The model continues each version once with greedy decoding. If the mention causes errors, B should end correct more often than C.

\paragraph{Experiment 2: resampling.}
Deleting text leaves a trace the model would not have written itself, and such off-policy edits can understate effects \citep{macar-etal-2026-branches}. So, following \citet{bogdan-etal-2025-anchors}, we also let the model regenerate seven continuations per trace at temperature 0.8 from four starting points. \emph{P} keeps the question and the sentences before $k$. \emph{Q} keeps the same sentences but removes the distractor from the question. \emph{M} also keeps sentence $k$, and \emph{P1} stops one sentence earlier than P. Q$-$P measures the distractor in the question, M$-$P the written mention, and (M$-$P)$-$(P$-$P1) whether the mention matters more than an ordinary sentence. Table~\ref{tab:intervention} shows every variant on one real trace.

\paragraph{Results of experiment 1.}
Deleting the mention helps no more than deleting a neutral sentence (+0.0 [$-$6.8, 7.2] on the 222 Hindi traces). With one continuation per version this test had about 24\% power for a 5-point effect, so it does not show the effect is zero.

\paragraph{Results of experiment 2.}
\textbf{Removing the distractor helps a little.} In Hindi, Q reaches 45.2\% and P 40.0\%, a difference of +5.3 points [3.2, 7.4] (Figure~\ref{fig:interventions}). So the distractor was necessary for at least 8.8\% of the errors made from that point \citep{tian-pearl-2000-probabilities}; most continuations that go wrong with it also go wrong without it. The effect leans on one sentence, again the one from Figure~\ref{fig:design}, which supplies 58\% of it (without it, +2.8 [0.4, 5.0]), and the interval reaches zero once the 29 sentences are treated as a sample.

\textbf{The mention itself looks like an ordinary step.} Keeping the sentence with the first mention lowers accuracy by 3.3 points [1.2, 5.4], but not detectably more than keeping the sentence before it ($p=0.09$ on 248 traces with $k\ge1$). It cannot be told apart from one more step along a trace that was heading for a wrong answer.

\textbf{The distractor and the act of writing about it stay entangled.} Free continuations mention the distractor in 57.8\% of rollouts, so the Q-versus-P contrast changes both at once. Unanswered Hindi rollouts also rise from 5.7\% to 10.0\%, which the API data does not show, so part of this may belong to the self-hosted setting.

\textbf{So the answer is no.} The distractor makes some errors more likely, but most recur without it, the effect depends on one sentence, and neither experiment isolates the written mention.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Across accuracy, trace distance, verification and error types, the instructed language of reasoning accounts for more of the gap than the language of the question, as prior work on English reasoning would predict \citep{shi-etal-2023-mgsm,yong2025crosslingual,qi-etal-2025-models}. Method rankings did not transfer: one English turn beat CLP's two, and a Hinglish instruction hurt English questions. Trace evidence needs care too: divergence goes with disagreement without showing cause, and a wrong trace that mentions a distractor needs a baseline.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On Sarvam-105B and TRD, CoT widens the English-Hindi gap from about 4 points to about 29. Reasoning in English on the same Hindi questions closes most of it, CLP a third and Hinglish none. The Hindi losses come from methods used only when writing Hindi; errors from reading Hindi survive the switch. Trace divergence tracks disagreement only as a correlation, and we cannot show that distractors in traces cause errors.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Scope.} Everything here concerns one model, greedy decoding except in the resampling arms, one prompt per condition, one dataset seed and two languages. Intervals cover question sampling only, so they say nothing about other wordings, and with temperature 0 there is no sampling noise estimate for the main runs.

\paragraph{Identification.} Language and prompt wording are confounded in every comparison across prompting conditions, including our decomposition. No condition pairs English questions with Hindi reasoning, so the split is one path and depends on its order. Trace style, mentions and verification are chosen by the model or measured after it responds, so they support association only. Runs span about a month, the provider changed its API responses in that time, and condition and run date are partly confounded.

\paragraph{Data.} The added conditions use the first 50 of 150 questions per cell. This subset is too small for distractor contrasts of a few points. Hindi distractors are not literal translations, and some Hindi carriers are awkward, so English-Hindi distractor comparisons are not strictly parallel. Distractor sentences are a fixed pool assigned unevenly across tasks, and the generator's seed reset makes levels share sentence mixes. TRD's time-task offsets in the code differ from the difficulty ranges its paper lists.

\paragraph{Measurement.} The parser is regex-based and imperfect, RDS depends on it and on the edit-distance costs, and RQ5 drops pairs with unparseable answers. Mention and verification rules are lexical. The error labels come from a script, leave about two-thirds of wrong standard responses unlabelled, and their percentages were not checked by hand. All our hand-labelled samples were read by a single LLM annotator without blinding or human validation, including parser precision and our review of the Hindi templates; they should be checked by people.

\paragraph{Interventions.} Both experiments cover only originally wrong traces and time-related distractors, on a quantised self-hosted model, and resampling uses a different temperature from the source traces. The resampling design was written down before the run according to our notes, but it was first committed afterwards, followed the deletion experiment on many of the same traces, and was not publicly registered. We did not test whether mentions harm traces that would otherwise be correct.

\paragraph{Thinking mode.} This pilot has 10 questions per cell and a fourfold budget, and it changes thinking and budget together.

\paragraph{Future work.} The most informative next steps are: rerunning recurrence with corrected Hindi carriers; adding English questions with Hindi reasoning to complete the design; an English CoT prompt without the language lock and several paraphrases of each prompt; CLP with solving forbidden in stage 1; thinking on and off at equal budgets; a question-by-mention factorial on both correct and wrong traces; and human annotation of the labelled samples and error labels.
