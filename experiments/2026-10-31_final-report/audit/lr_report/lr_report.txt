           Reasoning Divergence and Cross-Lingual Correctness:
                     A Logistic Regression Analysis



1     Question

Does a larger Reasoning Divergence Score (RDS) between English and Hindi reasoning traces
go with a higher chance that the two languages get different correctness outcomes on the same
question? All results come from existing Sarvam-105B outputs. No model was retrained or rerun.


2     Method

Outcome. For each English and Hindi question pair we define
                                {
                                  0 both correct or both incorrect,
                     disagree =
                                  1 exactly one language is correct.

Predictor. RDS is the normalized edit distance between the English and Hindi reasoning traces,
ranging from 0 (identical) to 1 (completely different).
Models. We fit logistic regressions with standard errors clustered by question, since each question
appears in up to three insertion variants and both reasoning modes.

    • Primary: disagree ∼ RDS + diﬀiculty + task + insertion type + reasoning mode.

    • Length-adjusted: the primary model plus a trace length gap, |ℓen − ℓhi |/ max(ℓen , ℓhi ).

    • Per-mode: the RDS slope estimated separately for reasoning OFF and reasoning ON.

Reference levels are short diﬀiculty, the date_addition task, no insertion, and reasoning OFF.
Sample. Of 21,600 loaded pairs, 938 (4.3%) were dropped because the English or Hindi answer
could not be parsed. The analysed sample has 20,662 pairs covering 6,739 unique questions:
19,471 with reasoning OFF and 1,191 with reasoning ON.


3     Results

3.1    How often do the languages disagree?

English and Hindi disagreed on 31.4% of pairs (95% CI [30.7, 32.0]). The disagreement is lopsided:
92% of cases are English correct and Hindi wrong.

It varies most by task: date_recurrence (65.1%) and day_of_week (55.2%) are highest, while

effect (30.7% to 32.2%).


3.2   Is RDS higher when the languages disagree?

Yes. Mean RDS is 0.809 when the languages agree and 0.917 when they disagree (medians 0.828
and 0.933). A Mann-Whitney test confirms the gap (p < 0.001): a randomly chosen disagreeing
pair has a higher RDS than a randomly chosen agreeing pair 80% of the time.

All RDS coeﬀicients are positive with p < 0.001.
Primary model. Holding diﬀiculty, task, insertion type and reasoning mode fixed, a 0.1 increase
in RDS multiplies the odds of disagreement by 4.93. On the probability scale this averages to
about 23 percentage points per 0.1 of RDS. The effect is not linear: predicted disagreement stays
near zero below RDS 0.7 and climbs steeply above 0.8 (Figure 2).
Length-adjusted model. Edit distance can grow just because one trace is longer. Adding the
length gap does not weaken the result: the RDS odds ratio rises slightly to 5.58. The length gap
itself has a negative coeﬀicient (β = −1.19, p < 0.001), so trace length differences do not explain
the association.
Reasoning ON vs OFF. Disagreement is far lower with reasoning ON (2.6%) than OFF (33.1%),
and reasoning ON lowers the adjusted odds of disagreement by about 90% (OR 0.10). The RDS
slope is positive in both modes. However, reasoning ON has only 31 disagreements. Its fully
adjusted model did not converge because the time_duration task had zero disagreements, so only
its RDS-only model should be read, and with caution.

4    Limitations

    • Observational. RDS and correctness come from the same generations, so we cannot say
      divergence causes errors. Both may reflect question diﬀiculty not captured by the controls.

    • Mode imbalance. Reasoning OFF makes up 94% of the pooled sample, so the pooled slope
      mostly describes reasoning OFF.

    • Asymmetric outcome. Since 92% of disagreements are Hindi failures, the outcome mainly
      tracks Hindi-specific errors.

    • Narrow RDS range. 88% of pairs have RDS above 0.7, so the slope is estimated mainly
      from the upper range.

    • Measurement. RDS depends on the step parser and edit-distance costs. Pairs with un-
      parseable answers were dropped.

    • In-sample. The models describe associations in this dataset, not predictive accuracy on new
      questions.


5    Conclusion

Greater divergence between English and Hindi reasoning traces is strongly associated with the
two languages reaching different correctness outcomes. The association holds after controlling for
diﬀiculty, task, insertion type, reasoning mode and trace length. RDS is therefore a useful signal of
cross-lingual reasoning inconsistency, though the analysis is correlational and does not show that
divergence causes the errors.
